Introduction to Basic Construction Materials

Classification and introductory concepts appearing in the class notes and reference material.

🏗️ Basic Construction Materials

Building Materials: Materials used for the construction of buildings and other civil-engineering structures are known as building materials or materials of construction.

Knowledge of the types, properties and uses of construction materials is important for selecting an economical, durable and suitable material for a particular engineering work.

Importance: Building materials constitute a major part of the total cost of construction. Therefore proper selection and efficient use of materials are important for economy, strength, function and appearance of structures.

🪨 Classification of Rocks — Foundation for Stones

Building stones are obtained from naturally occurring rocks. The class notes divide rocks according to their geological origin into three principal groups.

ROCKS → Igneous Rocks → Sedimentary Rocks → Metamorphic Rocks
1. Igneous Rocks

Formed by cooling and solidification of molten material such as magma or lava.

Examples: Granite, Basalt and Trap.

2. Sedimentary Rocks

Formed from deposited materials which subsequently become consolidated and cemented.

Examples: Sandstone, Shale, Limestone and Laterite.

3. Metamorphic Rocks

Produced when existing igneous or sedimentary rocks are changed by metamorphic action.

Examples: Quartzite, Schist, Slate, Marble and Gneiss.

🌋 Igneous Rocks

Igneous Rocks: Rocks formed by cooling and solidification of molten magma or lava.

The class notes associate igneous rocks with molten rock, volcanic activity, magma and solidification.

  • They are generally hard.
  • They are generally durable.
  • They are comparatively heavy.
  • They commonly possess a crystalline structure.
Molten Rock / Magma → Cooling → Solidification → Igneous Rock
Examples: Granite, Basalt and Trap.

🏜️ Sedimentary Rocks

Sedimentary Rocks: Rocks produced through deposition and subsequent consolidation or cementation of sediments.

The notes associate their formation with the action of natural agencies such as:

  • Sun
  • Air
  • Water

Sedimentary material is deposited and subsequently consolidated into rock.

Weathering / Disintegration → Transportation → Deposition → Consolidation / Cementation → Sedimentary Rock

Stratification

Sedimentary rocks commonly occur in layers. This layered arrangement is known as stratification.

Examples: Sandstone, Shale, Limestone and Laterite.

⛰️ Metamorphic Rocks

Metamorphic Rocks: Rocks formed when pre-existing igneous or sedimentary rocks are altered by the process of metamorphism.
Igneous / Sedimentary Rock → Metamorphism → Metamorphic Rock

The class notes identify metamorphic rocks as generally strong and durable and commonly possessing a foliated structure.

The thickness of foliation may range from a few centimetres to many metres in the reference notes.

Examples: Quartzite, Gneiss, Schist, Slate and Marble.

TOPIC 02

Building Stones

Classification, characteristics and requirements of stones used in civil engineering.

🪨 Building Stones

Building Stone: A natural rock which is suitable for use in construction after quarrying and, where required, cutting, dressing or polishing.

Building stones are obtained from naturally occurring rock formations.

According to the reference, approximately three-fourths of the Earth's land area is underlain by sedimentary rocks, while approximately one-fourth consists of igneous and metamorphic rocks.

Classification of Building Stones by Geological Origin

Igneous Granite, Basalt and Trap
Sedimentary Sandstone, Shale, Limestone and Laterite
Metamorphic Quartzite, Schist, Slate, Marble and Gneiss

⭐ Characteristics / Requirements of a Good Building Stone

A good building stone should possess suitable engineering properties so that it remains strong, durable and serviceable throughout the life of the structure.

1. High Strength

The stone should possess sufficient crushing or compressive strength.

The reference gives a desirable crushing strength greater than approximately 1000 kg/cm².

2. High Durability

It should withstand weathering and environmental action for a long period.

3. Sufficient Hardness

The stone should resist scratching, abrasion and wear.

The reference indicates a coefficient of hardness greater than approximately 14.

4. Resistance to Wear

Stone used in floors, pavements and road work should resist abrasion and repeated traffic action.

5. Fire Resistance

A good building stone should have adequate resistance against high temperature and fire.

6. Adequate Specific Gravity

The reference indicates that good building stone generally has a specific gravity greater than 2.7.

7. Crystalline Structure

A compact and sound crystalline structure is desirable for good strength and durability.

8. High Impact Value / Toughness

The stone should withstand impact and shock. The reference notes a toughness index greater than approximately 13.

9. Low Water Absorption

Water absorption should be low.

The reference gives water absorption after 24 hours as less than approximately 0.6%.

10. Ease of Dressing

Where architectural work is required, the stone should permit cutting, carving and dressing to the required shape.

11. Weather Resistance

Stone should withstand atmospheric and climatic conditions without rapid deterioration.

12. Good Appearance

For exposed and architectural work, colour, texture and appearance are important.

General observation: The reference states that stones obtained from igneous and metamorphic rocks are generally heavier and more durable than stones obtained from sedimentary rocks.

TOPIC 03

Quarrying of Stones

Meaning, methods of quarrying, wedging and blasting of rocks.

⛏️ Quarrying

Quarrying: The process of extracting stones from natural rock beds is called quarrying.

The place from which stones are obtained is known as a quarry.

Natural Rock Bed → Quarrying → Stone Blocks / Rock Pieces → Dressing → Construction Use

📚 Methods of Quarrying

The method selected depends upon the nature of the rock, its hardness, the required size of stone and the quantity of material to be extracted.

1. Digging / Excavation

Suitable for comparatively soft rock occurring at or near the ground surface.

2. Heating

Rock is heated so that unequal thermal expansion assists in separating portions of the rock.

3. Wedging

Holes are made along a required line and wedges are driven to split the rock.

4. Blasting

Used for hard and compact rock where large quantities are required.

1. Quarrying by Digging / Excavation

This method is used when the stone occurs in a comparatively soft formation and can be removed with ordinary tools.

Tools such as picks, crowbars and similar excavation tools may be used.

Quarrying by Digging — Concept

2. Quarrying by Heating

In this method the exposed surface of the rock is heated.

Because of unequal expansion of the heated portion, stresses are developed and the outer layer may separate from the main rock mass.

Heating of Rock Surface → Unequal Expansion → Development of Thermal Stresses → Separation of Rock Layer
Heating is applicable only where the nature of the rock and the required quarry product make the method suitable.

3. Quarrying by Wedging

Wedging is adopted when comparatively regular stone blocks are required and excessive fragmentation is undesirable.

General Procedure

  1. Mark the required line of separation on the rock.
  2. Make a series of holes along the marked line.
  3. Insert steel wedges or the plug-and-feather arrangement into the holes.
  4. Drive the wedges gradually and uniformly.
  5. Tensile stresses develop along the line of holes.
  6. The rock splits along the desired line.

🔨 Steel Point and Wedge

🔩 Plug and Feathers

Another arrangement shown in the reference consists of a central plug placed between two feathers.

When the plug is driven downward, the feathers exert lateral pressure on the sides of the hole and assist in splitting the rock.

Blasting: A quarrying method used for breaking hard and compact rock when extraction by simpler mechanical methods is not suitable.

TOPIC 04

Dressing & Polishing of Stones

Preparation of quarried stones to obtain the required shape, dimensions, surface and appearance.

🔨 Dressing of Stones

Dressing of Stone: The process of cutting, shaping and finishing a quarried stone to the required dimensions, shape and surface is called dressing.

Stones obtained directly from a quarry are generally irregular in shape and size. They are therefore dressed before being placed in the required construction work.

🎯 Objectives of Dressing

  • To obtain the required size of stone.
  • To obtain the required shape.
  • To provide proper beds and joints.
  • To remove irregular projections.
  • To remove weak portions where necessary.
  • To improve the appearance of exposed surfaces.
  • To obtain the required surface finish.
  • To facilitate proper construction and bonding.
  • To reduce unnecessary material and weight before transport or placement where practicable.

✨ Polishing of Stones

Polishing: Polishing is the finishing operation by which a smooth, even and, where required, glossy surface is produced on a suitable stone.

Polishing improves appearance and is particularly important for decorative stones such as marble and granite.

Purposes

  • Improve surface smoothness.
  • Improve appearance.
  • Bring out the colour and texture of the stone.
  • Provide a suitable architectural finish.
  • Facilitate cleaning of finished surfaces.

🏛️ Dressing According to Construction Requirement

The degree of dressing depends upon the location and purpose for which the stone is used.

Rough Work

Only projections and major irregularities are removed where a fine finish is unnecessary.

Masonry Work

Beds and joints are prepared so that stones can be properly placed and bonded.

Facing Work

The exposed face receives a more regular and attractive finish.

Architectural Work

Fine dressing, carving or polishing may be provided according to the required appearance.

TOPIC 05

Aggregates

Classification according to geological origin and size, maximum size of aggregate and basic aggregate terminology.

🪨 Aggregates

Aggregate: Aggregate is a granular material such as sand, gravel or crushed stone used as an important constituent of concrete and other construction works.

Aggregates occupy a major portion of the volume of concrete. Their geological origin, size, shape, surface texture, strength and other properties influence the behaviour of concrete.

📚 Classification of Aggregates

The reference notes classify aggregates on different bases. The important classifications covered here are:

AGGREGATES → Geological Origin → Size → Shape → Surface Texture → Other Engineering Properties

1. Classification According to Geological Origin

According to the type of parent rock, aggregates may be obtained from:

Igneous Rock Aggregates

Aggregates obtained from igneous rocks.

Examples of igneous parent rocks include granite, basalt and trap.

Sedimentary Rock Aggregates

Aggregates obtained from sedimentary rocks.

Examples of sedimentary parent rocks include sandstone and limestone.

Metamorphic Rock Aggregates

Aggregates obtained from metamorphic rocks.

Examples include suitable quartzite and other metamorphic rocks.

The properties of an aggregate are influenced by the mineral composition and structure of its parent rock.

2. Classification According to Size

Fine Aggregate

Aggregate passing through a 4.75 mm sieve is classified as fine aggregate.

Sand is the common example.

Coarse Aggregate

Aggregate retained on a 4.75 mm sieve is classified as coarse aggregate.

Gravel and crushed stone are common examples.

Important value to remember: 4.75 mm is the principal sieve size separating fine aggregate from coarse aggregate in the notes.

3. Maximum Size of Aggregate

Maximum Size of Aggregate: The maximum size represents the largest aggregate size permitted or present according to the specified grading and construction requirements.

Selection of the maximum size of aggregate is important because aggregate must be capable of passing through and being properly compacted within the structural member.

Selection Depends On

  • Dimensions of the structural member.
  • Thickness of the member.
  • Spacing between reinforcing bars.
  • Clear cover to reinforcement.
  • Method of mixing, transporting, placing and compacting concrete.
Aggregate should not be so large that it causes difficulty in placing concrete through reinforcement or prevents proper compaction.

Why is Aggregate Size Important?

The size of aggregate affects the total surface area that must be coated by cement paste.

Smaller Aggregate → Greater Total Surface Area → More Surface to be Coated by Paste
Larger Aggregate → Lower Total Surface Area → Potential Reduction in Paste Requirement

However, aggregate size cannot be increased without limit because structural dimensions, reinforcement spacing and proper concrete placement must also be considered.

TOPIC 06

Aggregate Shape, Angularity, Texture & Strength

Particle shape, angularity, surface texture, aggregate–cement paste bond and aggregate strength.

🔷 Shape of Aggregate

The shape of aggregate particles affects workability, interlocking, surface area and the behaviour of fresh and hardened concrete.

Shape Description General Effect
Rounded Particles having smooth and rounded surfaces, commonly produced by natural action. Lower inter-particle friction and generally better workability.
Irregular / Partly Rounded Particles having partly rounded and partly irregular surfaces. Behaviour lies between rounded and angular aggregate.
Angular Particles having comparatively sharp edges and angular faces. Greater interlocking, but generally lower workability than rounded aggregate.
Flaky Particles whose thickness is small compared with their other dimensions. Excessive flaky particles are undesirable.
Elongated Particles whose length is considerably greater than their other dimensions. Excessive elongated particles are undesirable.
Flaky and Elongated Particles possessing both undesirable shape characteristics. Generally unsuitable when present in excessive quantity.

🎨 Aggregate Shapes — Diagram

Common Aggregate Particle Shapes

📐 Flaky Aggregate

A particle is described as flaky when its least dimension or thickness is small in relation to its mean size.

Flaky particles tend to orient themselves during placing and may adversely affect packing and concrete quality when present in excessive quantity.

📏 Elongated Aggregate

A particle is described as elongated when its greatest dimension or length is large in relation to its mean size.

Excessive elongated particles are generally avoided because particle shape influences workability, compaction and aggregate packing.

🔺 Angularity of Aggregate

Angularity: Angularity indicates the degree to which an aggregate particle differs from a rounded form and possesses edges or angular surfaces.

Angular aggregate generally develops greater interlocking between particles.

Rounded aggregate generally produces less internal friction and therefore better workability.

More Rounded → Less Inter-Particle Friction → Better Workability
More Angular → Greater Interlocking → Greater Resistance to Movement

🖐️ Surface Texture of Aggregate

Surface Texture: Surface texture describes the degree of smoothness, roughness or porosity of the surface of an aggregate particle.

Aggregate surfaces may be relatively smooth, rough, granular, crystalline or porous depending upon the parent rock and the process by which the aggregate was formed.

Smooth Surface

Generally provides less mechanical interlocking with cement paste.

Rough Surface

Can provide greater mechanical interlocking and improved bond with cement paste.

🔗 Aggregate–Cement Paste Bond

The bond between aggregate and cement paste is an important factor in concrete.

The bond is influenced by:

  • Surface texture of aggregate.
  • Surface roughness.
  • Cleanliness of aggregate.
  • Particle shape.
  • Characteristics of the cement paste.
Clean Aggregate Surface + Suitable Surface Texture + Cement Paste → Aggregate–Paste Bond
Dust, clay or other coatings on aggregate surfaces may interfere with proper bond between the aggregate and cement paste.

💪 Strength of Aggregate

Aggregate should possess sufficient strength to resist the stresses developed during construction and during the service life of concrete.

Aggregate strength is particularly important because weak aggregate may limit the strength and durability of concrete.

Important Strength-Related Properties

  • Resistance to crushing.
  • Resistance to impact.
  • Resistance to abrasion and wear.
  • Toughness.
  • Durability.

Remember: There are nine aggregate tests in this reference list.

1. Aggregate Crushing Value Test

Aggregate Crushing Value (ACV) indicates the resistance of aggregate to crushing under a gradually applied compressive load.

The test is used as an indication of the crushing strength of aggregate.

Aggregate Crushing Value

ACV = (Weight of fines produced / Original weight of sample) × 100
A lower aggregate crushing value indicates greater resistance to crushing.

2. Aggregate Impact Value Test

Aggregate Impact Value (AIV) indicates the resistance of aggregate to sudden impact or shock.
Aggregate Impact Value

AIV = (Weight of fines produced after impact / Original weight of sample) × 100

The test gives an indication of the toughness of aggregate.

Lower impact value generally indicates greater resistance to impact.

3. Aggregate Abrasion Value Test

The abrasion test determines the resistance of aggregate to wear and abrasion.

Abrasion resistance is particularly important for aggregates used in surfaces subjected to traffic, rubbing or repeated wear.

Aggregate → Abrasive Action → Measurement of Wear / Material Loss → Abrasion Resistance

4. Specific Gravity Test

Specific Gravity: The ratio of the density or unit weight of a material to the density or unit weight of water at the specified reference condition.
Specific Gravity = Density of Aggregate / Density of Water

Specific gravity is used in concrete mix calculations and in evaluating aggregate properties.

5. Bulk Density Test

Bulk Density: The mass of aggregate occupying a unit bulk volume, including the volume of voids between particles.
Bulk Density = Mass of Aggregate / Bulk Volume Occupied

Bulk density depends upon particle size, grading, shape and the degree of compaction.

6. Water Absorption & Moisture Content

Aggregates may contain water within their pores or on their surfaces.

Water Absorption

Water absorption indicates the amount of water absorbed by aggregate relative to its dry mass.
Water Absorption (%) = [(Saturated mass − Dry mass) / Dry mass] × 100

Moisture Content

Moisture content represents the quantity of water present in an aggregate sample relative to its dry mass.
Moisture Content (%) = [(Wet mass − Dry mass) / Dry mass] × 100
Moisture present in aggregate affects the actual amount of water introduced into a concrete mix.

7. Fineness Modulus

Fineness Modulus (FM) is an empirical index indicating the overall coarseness or fineness of an aggregate sample.
Fineness Modulus = Sum of Cumulative Percentage Retained on the Specified Standard Sieves / 100
A higher fineness modulus represents a comparatively coarser aggregate grading, while a lower value represents a comparatively finer grading.

8. Flakiness Index

Flakiness Index: The percentage by mass of flaky particles present in an aggregate sample.
Flakiness Index (%) = Weight of Flaky Particles / Total Weight of Sample × 100

The test is used to determine the proportion of particles having an undesirable flaky shape.

9. Elongation Index

Elongation Index: The percentage by mass of elongated particles present in an aggregate sample.
Elongation Index (%) = Weight of Elongated Particles / Total Weight of Sample × 100

The test indicates the proportion of excessively elongated particles in an aggregate sample.

Bricks

Brick earth, manufacture of bricks, classification, properties, tests and uses of bricks in construction.

🧱 Bricks

Brick: A brick is an artificial building unit manufactured mainly from suitable clay, moulded to the required shape and size, dried and burnt.

Bricks are among the most commonly used construction materials for walls, partitions and other masonry works.

Suitable Brick Earth → Preparation → Moulding → Drying → Burning → Finished Bricks

🌍 Constituents of Good Brick Earth

Suitable brick earth contains constituents that contribute to mouldability, strength, hardness and proper burning.

1. Alumina

Provides plasticity to brick earth and permits the material to be moulded into the required shape.

Excess alumina may cause excessive shrinkage and warping.

2. Silica

Helps prevent excessive shrinkage and cracking and contributes to the strength and durability of bricks.

Excess silica may reduce cohesion and make the brick brittle.

3. Lime

In suitable quantity, lime assists fusion during burning and contributes to strength.

Lime should be finely divided and uniformly distributed.

4. Iron Oxide

Iron oxide contributes to the colour of burnt bricks and assists the burning process.

5. Magnesia

A small quantity of magnesia may contribute to the colour of bricks and can reduce shrinkage.

⚠️ Harmful Ingredients in Brick Earth

Certain materials are undesirable when present in excessive quantities because they can produce defects in finished bricks.

  • Lime nodules: may expand after burning when exposed to moisture and can damage the brick.
  • Iron pyrites: may produce disintegration or staining.
  • Alkalies: may contribute to efflorescence and deformation during burning.
  • Pebbles and gravel: interfere with uniform mixing and moulding.
  • Organic matter: may burn away and leave pores or cavities.

🏭 Manufacture of Bricks

The manufacture of clay bricks can be divided into four principal stages.

1. Preparation of Brick Earth → 2. Moulding → 3. Drying → 4. Burning

1. Preparation of Brick Earth

Brick earth is prepared before moulding so that a uniform and workable clay mass is obtained.

Operations in Preparation

  1. Unsoiling
  2. Digging
  3. Cleaning
  4. Weathering
  5. Blending
  6. Tempering

Unsoiling

The upper layer of soil containing vegetation, organic matter and other undesirable material is removed before useful brick earth is excavated.

Digging

After removal of the top soil, suitable clay is dug out and collected for further preparation.

Cleaning

Stones, pebbles, roots, vegetable matter and other undesirable materials are removed from the excavated earth.

Weathering

Weathering: The excavated brick earth is exposed to atmospheric action for a suitable period so that the material becomes more suitable for subsequent processing.

Blending

The required ingredients are mixed with the brick earth in suitable proportions to obtain a uniform composition.

Tempering

Tempering: The process of adding water to prepared brick earth and thoroughly mixing or kneading it to obtain a homogeneous plastic mass suitable for moulding.
Prepared Clay + Water → Thorough Mixing / Kneading → Plastic Brick Earth

2. Moulding of Bricks

Moulding: The process of giving prepared brick earth the required shape and dimensions is called moulding.

Moulding may broadly be carried out by:

Hand Moulding

Bricks are moulded manually using brick moulds.

Machine Moulding

Mechanical equipment is used to mould bricks, particularly where large-scale production is required.

✋ Hand Moulding

Hand moulding is generally divided into:

  1. Ground moulding
  2. Table moulding

Ground Moulding

The mould is placed on properly prepared ground and filled with tempered clay.

Table Moulding

The moulding operation is performed on a moulding table, providing a more convenient working surface.

⚙️ Machine Moulding

Machine moulding is suitable for large-scale brick production and provides comparatively uniform bricks.

Machine moulding methods may include:

  • Plastic clay process.
  • Dry or stiff-mud pressing process, depending upon the manufacturing system.

Freshly moulded bricks contain moisture and cannot be burnt immediately.

They are therefore dried before burning.

Purposes of Drying

  • Remove moisture from green bricks.

Burning: The dried bricks are heated under controlled conditions to develop the required strength, hardness, durability and colour.

Proper burning is one of the most important stages in brick manufacture.

Bricks may be burnt using:

Specially constructed arrangements used for more controlled and efficient burning.

📚 Classification of Bricks

Bricks may broadly be classified as:

BRICKS → Unburnt Bricks + Burnt Bricks

Unburnt Bricks

These bricks are dried but are not burnt. They are generally suitable only for temporary or low-grade construction where conditions permit.

Burnt Bricks

Burnt bricks are commonly divided according to their quality into:

  1. First-class bricks
  2. Second-class bricks
  3. Third-class bricks
  4. Fourth-class bricks

🥇 First-Class Bricks

  • Well burnt.
  • Uniform in shape and size.
  • Regular rectangular form.
  • Sharp and straight edges.
  • Hard and sound.
  • Comparatively free from cracks and major defects.
  • Suitable for superior and important construction work.

🥈 Second-Class Bricks

  • Burnt bricks of comparatively lower quality than first-class bricks.
  • May have slight irregularities in shape or surface.
  • Suitable for ordinary construction, particularly where the masonry is to be plastered.

🥉 Third-Class Bricks

  • Under-burnt or comparatively soft.
  • Lower strength and durability.
  • More susceptible to moisture.
  • Generally used only for temporary construction in suitable dry locations.

4️⃣ Fourth-Class Bricks

  • Over-burnt and often distorted.
  • Not suitable for normal brick masonry.
  • Broken material may be used for suitable filling or aggregate-type applications where permitted.

⭐ Characteristics of Good Bricks

A good building brick should have suitable strength, shape, durability and resistance to environmental action.

  • It should have a uniform colour.
  • It should have a regular shape and size.
  • Edges should be sharp and straight.
  • Faces should be reasonably even.
  • It should be hard and compact.
  • It should be free from visible cracks, stones and harmful impurities.
  • It should produce a clear ringing sound when two sound bricks are struck together.
  • It should possess adequate compressive strength.
  • Water absorption should be limited.
  • Efflorescence should be absent or within acceptable limits.
  • It should be durable against normal weather exposure.

🧪 Tests on Bricks

Important tests used for assessing brick quality include:

  1. Compressive strength test
  2. Water absorption test
  3. Efflorescence test
  4. Hardness test
  5. Soundness test
  6. Shape and size test
  7. Structure test

1. Compressive Strength Test

This test determines the resistance of a brick to compressive load.
Compressive Strength

σc = Maximum Compressive Load / Loaded Area

The result is normally expressed as force per unit area.

2. Water Absorption Test

The water absorption test determines the amount of water absorbed by a brick relative to its dry mass.

Water Absorption (%)

= [(W2 − W1) / W1] × 100

Where:

  • W1 = dry weight of brick
  • W2 = weight of brick after water absorption

3. Efflorescence Test

Efflorescence: The appearance of whitish salt deposits on the surface of bricks due to soluble salts is called efflorescence.

The test is used to assess the presence and degree of soluble salts in bricks.

Excessive efflorescence is undesirable because it can spoil appearance and indicates the presence of soluble salts.

4. Hardness Test

The surface of the brick is examined for resistance to scratching.

A good brick should be sufficiently hard and should not be easily scratched.

5. Soundness Test

Two bricks are struck against each other.

A good sound brick should produce a clear ringing sound and should not break easily under the simple field check.

6. Shape and Size Test

Bricks are checked for uniformity in dimensions, straightness of edges and regularity of shape.

Good bricks should be rectangular and reasonably uniform in size.

7. Structure Test

A brick is broken and its internal structure is examined.

A good brick should show a compact and homogeneous structure and should be free from major voids, lumps and harmful inclusions.

Timber

Timber as a building material, structure of timber, characteristics, uses, seasoning, preservation and defects in timber.

🌳 Timber

Timber: Timber is wood obtained from trees and converted into a form suitable for engineering and building construction purposes.

Timber has been used as a construction material for structural, architectural and temporary works because it is comparatively light, workable and capable of providing a good appearance.

Tree → Felling → Conversion → Seasoning → Preservation where required → Usable Timber

🌲 Classification of Trees

Trees supplying timber can broadly be classified according to their mode of growth.

1. Endogenous Trees

These trees grow inward and have a fibrous internal structure.

Examples commonly associated with this group include bamboo and palms.

2. Exogenous Trees

These trees grow outward by the formation of successive annual rings.

Most important structural timbers are obtained from exogenous trees.

🍃 Exogenous Trees

Exogenous trees may further be grouped as:

Coniferous Trees

Generally evergreen trees with needle-like leaves. They commonly yield softwood.

Examples include pine, fir and deodar.

Deciduous Trees

Trees which generally shed their leaves seasonally. They commonly yield hardwood.

Examples include teak and sal.

The terms softwood and hardwood are botanical classifications and do not necessarily indicate the actual physical hardness of every timber species.

🪵 Structure of Timber

A cross-section of the trunk of an exogenous tree shows several distinct parts.

  1. Bark
  2. Cambium layer
  3. Sapwood
  4. Heartwood
  5. Pith or medulla
  6. Annual rings
  7. Medullary rays

☀️ Seasoning of Timber

Seasoning: Seasoning is the controlled process of reducing the moisture content of freshly cut timber to a suitable level before its use in construction.

Freshly felled timber contains considerable moisture. If it is used without suitable drying, dimensional changes and other defects may develop.

Green Timber → Removal of Excess Moisture → Seasoned Timber → Improved Suitability for Use

🎯 Objectives / Advantages of Seasoning

  • To reduce moisture content.
  • To reduce the weight of timber.
  • To improve dimensional stability.
  • To minimise excessive shrinkage after use.
  • To reduce the possibility of warping and splitting.
  • To improve strength characteristics associated with properly dried timber.
  • To improve workability.
  • To improve the effectiveness of paints, finishes and preservative treatments.
  • To reduce conditions favourable to fungal decay.

📚 Methods of Seasoning

Seasoning methods may broadly be classified into:

SEASONING → Natural Seasoning + Artificial Seasoning

1. Natural Seasoning

Natural seasoning relies mainly upon natural atmospheric conditions for removal of moisture.

Air Seasoning

Timber sections are stacked so that air can circulate around them and gradually remove moisture.

Water Seasoning

Timber may first be subjected to water treatment to remove sap and is subsequently dried.

Artificial methods provide greater control over the conditions under which moisture is removed.

  • Electrical seasoning.
  • Other controlled drying processes depending upon the required timber product.

🔥 Kiln Seasoning

Kiln Seasoning: Timber is dried in a specially designed chamber in which temperature, humidity and air circulation can be controlled.

Advantages

  • Faster than ordinary air seasoning.
  • Drying conditions can be controlled.
  • More uniform moisture reduction can be achieved when properly operated.

🛡️ Preservation of Timber

Preservation of Timber: The treatment of timber to increase its resistance to deterioration caused by fungi, insects, moisture and other destructive agencies is called preservation.

🎯 Objectives of Preservation

  • Increase the service life of timber.
  • Protect timber against fungal decay.
  • Protect timber against insects and termites.
  • Improve resistance to deterioration in exposed conditions.
  • Reduce maintenance and replacement requirements.

🧪 Timber Preservatives

Preservatives may be selected according to the species of timber, exposure condition and intended use.

Common categories include:

  • Oil-type preservatives.
  • Water-borne preservatives.
  • Other suitable protective chemical treatments.

Spraying

Pressure Treatment

Preservative is introduced into timber under controlled pressure to obtain deeper penetration.

⚠️ Defects in Timber

Defect in Timber: Any irregularity or imperfection that adversely affects the strength, durability, appearance or usefulness of timber may be regarded as a defect.

Defects may arise due to:

  • Natural growth of the tree.
  • Conversion of timber.
  • Improper seasoning.
  • Fungal attack.
  • Insect attack.

Case Hardening

🍄 Defects Due to Fungi

Fungi may attack timber when suitable moisture, temperature and environmental conditions are present.

Fungal defects may include:

  • Wet rot.
  • Sap or blue stain.
  • Other forms of fungal decay and discolouration.

Dry Rot

Dry rot is a serious fungal deterioration of timber that damages the wood structure and can result in substantial loss of strength.

Good ventilation, control of moisture and appropriate preservative treatment help reduce conditions that promote fungal decay.

Wet Rot

Wet rot is fungal deterioration associated with timber remaining under persistently damp or wet conditions.

Timber may be attacked by insects that bore into or consume the wood.

Important examples include:

  • Termites.
  • Beetles and wood-boring insects.
  • Marine borers where timber is exposed to marine conditions.

Termite Attack

Termites can attack timber and create internal passages, causing progressive loss of material and strength.

Suitable preservation and protection of timber from termite access are therefore important in susceptible locations.

Define timber.

Answer: Timber is wood obtained from trees and converted into a form suitable for engineering and building construction.
5 MARKS

Explain the structure of timber.

Answer: The important parts visible in the cross-section of an exogenous tree include bark, cambium layer, sapwood, heartwood, pith, annual rings and medullary rays. The bark protects the tree externally. The cambium layer produces new growth. Sapwood is the younger outer wood, while heartwood is the mature inner wood. The pith forms the central core. Annual rings indicate successive growth, and medullary rays extend radially through the wood.
5 MARKS

State the characteristics of good timber.

Answer: Good timber should possess adequate strength, hardness, toughness and durability. It should be reasonably straight grained, workable, dimensionally stable after seasoning and free from serious defects. It should also have suitable resistance to decay and insect attack for its intended use.
5 MARKS

Write the uses of timber as a building material.

Answer: Timber is used for doors, windows, frames, roof members, flooring, stairs, partitions, furniture, interior finishing, temporary works, formwork, scaffolding components, poles, posts, sleepers and structural or decorative construction.
2 MARKS

Define seasoning of timber.

Answer: Seasoning is the controlled process of reducing the moisture content of freshly cut timber to a suitable level before use.
5 MARKS

State the objectives of seasoning.

Answer: The objectives of seasoning are to reduce moisture and weight, improve dimensional stability, minimise shrinkage and warping, improve workability and provide better conditions for finishing and preservation.
5 MARKS

Classify the methods of seasoning.

Answer: Seasoning is broadly classified into:
  1. Natural seasoning: air seasoning and water seasoning.
  2. Artificial seasoning: kiln seasoning, chemical seasoning, electrical seasoning and other controlled drying processes.
5 MARKS

Explain kiln seasoning.

Answer: In kiln seasoning, timber is placed in a specially designed chamber where temperature, humidity and air circulation are controlled. This permits faster and more controlled drying than ordinary natural seasoning.
2 MARKS

Define preservation of timber.

Answer: Preservation is the treatment of timber to improve its resistance to deterioration caused by fungi, insects, moisture and other destructive agencies.
5 MARKS

Explain the methods of applying timber preservatives.

Answer: Preservatives may be applied by brushing or painting, spraying, dipping or immersion and pressure treatment. Pressure treatment is used where deeper penetration of the preservative into timber is required.
10 MARKS

Explain the defects in timber.

Answer: Timber defects can be classified according to their origin.

1. Natural or growth defects: knots, shakes, twisted fibres, rind galls and burls.

2. Conversion defects: diagonal grain, torn grain and chip marks.

3. Seasoning defects: checks, splits, bow, cup, spring, twist, honeycombing and case hardening.

4. Fungal defects: dry rot, wet rot and other fungal decay or stains.

5. Insect defects: termite attack, beetles and other wood-boring insects.
5 MARKS

Explain knots and shakes in timber.

Answer: A knot is the portion of a branch embedded in the main wood. It interrupts the normal continuity of fibres and may reduce strength.

Shakes are separations or cracks within the wood structure. Important forms include heart shake, star shake and ring or cup shake.
5 MARKS

Explain the common forms of warping in timber.

Answer: Warping is distortion caused mainly by unequal shrinkage during drying. The common forms are:
  • Bow
  • Cup
  • Spring or crook
  • Twist

Laminates

Meaning, manufacture, properties and uses of laminates in building construction.

🪵 Laminates

Laminate: A laminate is a material manufactured by bonding together two or more layers of material using a suitable adhesive or bonding agent to obtain a composite product having the required properties.

In building construction, laminated products are commonly used for furniture, partitions, wall finishes, doors, cabinets and decorative surfaces.

Individual Layers + Adhesive / Bonding Material → Pressure and Suitable Processing → Laminated Product

🔗 Principle of Lamination

Instead of using a single thick piece of material, thin layers are bonded together to form a composite member or sheet.

The properties of the final laminate depend upon:

  • Type of layers used.
  • Number and thickness of layers.
  • Direction or arrangement of layers.
  • Type of adhesive or resin.
  • Pressure used during manufacture.
  • Temperature and curing conditions where applicable.

TOPIC 11

Scaffolding

Meaning, components, requirements and common types of scaffolding used during construction.

🏗️ Scaffolding

Scaffolding: Scaffolding is a temporary structure erected to provide working platforms and access for workers and materials during construction, repair, maintenance or finishing work at height.
Scaffolding is a temporary construction arrangement and should provide adequate stability and safe access for the intended work.

🔩 Main Components of Scaffolding

Component Function
Standards Vertical members that transfer loads towards the ground or supporting base.
Ledgers Horizontal members running parallel to the wall and connecting the standards.
Putlogs / Transoms Transverse members supporting the working platform.
Braces Diagonal members used to improve stiffness and stability.
Working Platform Platform on which workers stand and carry out the construction operation.
Guard Rails Protective members provided at exposed platform edges as part of a safe scaffold arrangement.
Toe Boards Boards provided at platform edges to help prevent materials or tools from falling.

⭐ Requirements of Good Scaffolding

  • Adequate strength for the intended loads.
  • Adequate stability.
  • Properly supported standards and members.
  • Adequate bracing.
  • Suitable working platforms.
  • Safe access between working levels.
  • Proper connections between members.
  • Appropriate edge protection where required.
  • Regular inspection and maintenance during use.

📚 Types of Scaffolding

Common forms of scaffolding include:

  1. Single scaffolding.
  2. Double scaffolding.
  3. Cantilever scaffolding.
  4. Suspended scaffolding.
  5. Trestle scaffolding.
  6. Steel or tubular scaffolding.
  7. Patented / prefabricated scaffolding systems.

1. Single Scaffolding

Single scaffolding has one main row of standards parallel to the wall.

It is traditionally associated with brick masonry work where the wall can provide support for the inner ends of putlogs.

2. Double Scaffolding

Double scaffolding has two rows of standards and is constructed independently of direct putlog support from the wall.

It is commonly associated with stone masonry or situations where making openings in the wall for putlogs is unsuitable.

Double scaffolding is also commonly called independent scaffolding.

3. Cantilever Scaffolding

In cantilever scaffolding, the working structure is supported by members projecting from or supported by the building rather than by ordinary standards extending directly to the ground at the working face.

It may be considered where ground conditions or obstructions make conventional support difficult.

4. Suspended Scaffolding

In suspended scaffolding, the working platform is suspended from an overhead structure using suitable supporting arrangements.

It is used for suitable work on tall building faces, maintenance, painting and repair operations.

5. Trestle Scaffolding

The working platform is supported on movable ladders, tripods or trestles.

It is generally associated with work at comparatively limited heights, particularly internal finishing or maintenance.

6. Steel / Tubular Scaffolding

Steel tubes or similar metallic members are connected using appropriate couplers and fittings.

General Advantages

  • Good strength.
  • Durability.
  • Reusability.
  • Comparatively rapid assembly when using suitable systems.
  • Consistent member dimensions.

TOPIC 12

Formwork

Definition, requirements, components, materials and formwork for common reinforced-concrete members.

🏗️ Formwork

Formwork: Formwork is a temporary or permanent mould into which fresh concrete is placed and supported until the concrete develops sufficient strength to maintain the required shape.

Formwork is used to obtain the required dimensions, shape, alignment and surface finish of concrete members.

Formwork Erected → Reinforcement Positioned → Fresh Concrete Placed → Concrete Hardens → Formwork Removed when Appropriate

Falsework

Falsework: Temporary supporting construction used to carry formwork and construction loads until the permanent structure becomes capable of supporting itself.
Formwork provides the mould or surface that gives concrete its required shape.

Falsework provides temporary structural support to the formwork and construction loads.

⭐ Requirements of Good Formwork

Good formwork should:

  • Possess sufficient strength.
  • Possess adequate rigidity.
  • Remain stable during concrete placement.
  • Maintain the required line, level and dimensions.
  • Have sufficiently tight joints to limit loss of cement paste or grout.
  • Provide the required surface finish.
  • Permit erection and removal without unnecessary damage to the concrete.
  • Be economical for the intended number of uses.
  • Be reasonably easy to handle and assemble.
  • Allow suitable access for placing and compacting concrete.

⚖️ Loads Acting on Formwork

Formwork and its supporting system may be required to resist:

  • Self-weight of formwork.
  • Weight of reinforcement.
  • Weight and pressure of fresh concrete.
  • Construction workers and equipment.
  • Loads produced during placing and compaction.
  • Other construction loads applicable to the work.

🧱 Materials Used for Formwork

Timber

Traditional material that can be easily cut and shaped.

Plywood

Provides comparatively large smooth surfaces and is commonly used as form-facing material.

Steel

Strong, durable and suitable for repeated use when properly maintained.

Aluminium

Lightweight metallic formwork used in suitable repetitive construction systems.

Plastic / Composite Systems

Reusable modular formwork systems may use plastics or composite materials.

🔩 Basic Formwork Components

Depending upon the member being cast, a formwork system may include:

  • Sheathing or form face.
  • Studs.
  • Joists.
  • Walers.
  • Props or shores.
  • Braces.
  • Ties.
  • Clamps and connectors.

Column Formwork

Column formwork encloses the sides of a column and maintains its required cross-section, alignment and verticality while concrete is placed and hardened.

The form faces are held together using suitable clamps, ties or supporting arrangements.

Beam Formwork

Beam formwork normally provides:

  • A soffit or bottom form.
  • Side forms.
  • Supporting joists or bearers.
  • Props or shores.
  • Bracing where required.

The system should maintain the required beam dimensions and alignment while supporting fresh concrete and construction loads.

Wall Formwork

Wall formwork generally consists of form faces on opposite sides of the wall, supported by studs, walers, ties and bracing.

The forms maintain the required wall thickness, alignment and surface while fresh concrete is placed.

🔧 Removal of Formwork

Stripping / De-shuttering: The removal of formwork after concrete has developed sufficient strength for the particular member and construction condition.

Formwork should not be removed prematurely. Removal should be carried out so that the concrete member is not subjected to damaging loads, shocks or excessive disturbance.

Actual stripping time depends on factors such as the member, concrete strength development, span, loading, temperature, cementitious system and applicable construction specification.
