01

General & History

History and development of cementing materials and Portland cement.

General

  • Cementing materials have been used from ancient times.
  • Early Egyptian records mention materials obtained by burning gypsum.
  • Greeks and Romans used lime and hydraulic cementing materials.

Roman Practice

  • Roman builders mixed volcanic materials with lime.
  • Volcanic tuff was used with lime to obtain good strength and durability.
  • Pozzolana: Natural pozzolanic material obtained near Mount Vesuvius.
  • Artificial pozzolanic materials included powdered tiles and pottery.
  • Surkhi: Powdered brick used as a pozzolanic material.
  • Roman lime mortar was mixed and long-rammed, sometimes with or without Surkhi.

John Smeaton

  • Investigated hydraulic lime.
  • His work showed the importance of clayey limestone.
  • His work during construction of the Eddystone Lighthouse helped establish the importance of hydraulic cementing materials.

L. J. Vicat

  • Investigated artificial hydraulic lime.
  • Calcined mixtures of limestone and clay.

Joseph Aspdin and Portland Cement

  • Joseph Aspdin is credited with the invention of Portland cement.
  • Patent date: 21 October 1824.
  • The hardened cement resembled natural stone from Portland, England.

Development in India

  • Early attempt near Madras: 1904.
  • Indian Cement Company established at Porbandar: 1912–1913.
  • By 1918, three factories together produced about 85,000 tonnes/year.

Wet Process

Preparation and burning of slurry.

Wet Process

  • Limestone and clay are crushed.
  • They may be washed where necessary.
  • They are wet-ground with water to form slurry.
  • Water in slurry: about 35–50%.

Slurry Preparation

  • Wet grinding gives a homogeneous slurry.
  • Composition is tested and corrected periodically.
  • Corrected slurry is stored in large tanks.
  • Agitation keeps the slurry homogeneous.

Burning

  • Slurry enters the rotary kiln.
  • Fuel may be powdered coal, oil or gas.
  • Water is removed as the material moves through the kiln.
  • Chemical reactions occur and clinker is formed.
Main disadvantage: A large amount of water must be evaporated, so the wet process requires more fuel than the dry process.

Dry Process

Preparation of dry raw meal and modern dry-process manufacture.

Dry Process

  • Raw materials are crushed dry.
  • They are proportioned and fed to grinding mills.
  • Materials are dried and reduced to a fine powder.
  • The powder is called raw meal.
  • Raw meal is blended and corrected before entering the kiln.

Raw Meal Handling

  • Proportioned raw material is transported to the raw mill.
  • Grinding produces powdered raw mix.
  • Raw mix is stored in the raw meal silo.
  • Blending takes place in the silo.

Semi-dry Process

  • Blended dry meal is partly mixed with water.
  • It is formed into nodules/pellets in a disc granulator.
  • Nodules allow air flow for heat exchange and further reactions.

Modern Dry Process Features

  • Computerised control.
  • Online analysers.
  • Precalciners.
  • Vertical roller mills.
  • Stacker-reclaimers.
  • Process monitoring.
  • Automated sampling and laboratory analysis.

05

Rotary Kiln

Burning, clinker formation and cooling.

Rotary Kiln

  • A refractory-lined rotating steel cylinder.
  • Raw material is heated and converted into clinker inside the kiln.
  • Corrected slurry or dry raw meal enters the kiln.
  • Material moves gradually towards the hot zone.
  • Fuel may be powdered coal, oil or natural gas.

Rotary Kiln — Simple View

Raw meal / slurry
ROTARY KILN
Refractory lining
Hot zone
≈ 1500°C
Clinker

Burning and Clinker Formation

  • Temperature in the hottest part: about 1500°C.
  • About 20–30% of material gets fused.
  • Lime, silica and alumina react and combine.
  • Fused mass forms nodular clinker.
  • Clinker nodule size: about 3–20 mm.

Clinker Cooling

  • Clinker is cooled in a rotary cooler.
  • Clinker leaving the kiln may be about 1200°C.
  • It may be reduced to about 500°C within about 15 minutes.
  • Cooling rate affects crystallisation, crystal size and clinker properties.

Grinding and Packing

  • Clinker is ground with about 2–3% gypsum in a ball mill.
  • Gypsum is added to prevent flash setting.
  • Ground cement is stored in silos and packed.

Hydration of Cement

Hydration mechanisms and products.

Meaning of Hydration

  • Anhydrous cement does not bind fine and coarse aggregate by itself.
  • It develops adhesive properties when mixed with water.
  • Cement compounds react chemically with water.
  • Hydrated products are formed.

Hydration Sequence

Two Mechanisms

  • Through-solution: Cement compounds dissolve in water → hydrated products form → products precipitate.
  • Solid-state: Water attacks cement compounds in the solid state → products develop from the surface inward.
  • Through-solution is predominant in early hydration when large quantities of water are available.
  • Both mechanisms may operate during later hydration.

Hydration Products

Cement compounds + Water → C-S-H gel + Ca(OH)₂ + Aluminate hydrates
  • Hydration products form the binding phases responsible for hardening and strength development.

Heat evolution during cement hydration.

Meaning

  • Reaction of cement with water is exothermic.
  • It releases considerable heat.
  • Heat can be observed by comparing cement-paste temperature with the surroundings.

Calcium Silicate Hydrates

C-S-H, C₃S/C₂S hydration, strength and calcium hydroxide.

Calcium Silicate Hydrates — C-S-H

  • C₃S and C₂S react with water and form calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H).
  • C-S-H occupies a large part of the solid volume in completely hydrated paste.
  • C-S-H is the principal binding product.

C₃S Hydration

2(3CaO·SiO₂) + 6H₂O → 3CaO·2SiO₂·3H₂O + 3Ca(OH)₂
2C₃S + 6H → C₃S₂H₃ + 3Ca(OH)₂
  • C₃S hydrates comparatively rapidly.
  • Main contribution: early strength.
  • C₃S-rich cement can be useful when early strength is important.

C₂S Hydration

2(2CaO·SiO₂) + 4H₂O → 3CaO·2SiO₂·3H₂O + Ca(OH)₂
2C₂S + 4H → C₃S₂H₃ + Ca(OH)₂
  • C₂S hydrates more slowly.
  • Main contribution: later strength.
  • It produces less Ca(OH)₂ than C₃S.

C₃S vs C₂S — Easy Comparison

C₃SC₂S
Faster hydrationSlower hydration
Early strengthLater strength
More Ca(OH)₂Less Ca(OH)₂

Calcium Hydroxide

  • Another major hydration product.
  • Occupies about 20–25% of the volume of solids in hydrated paste.
  • Comparatively weak.
  • Can be attacked by sulphates.
  • Fly ash and silica fume can consume calcium hydroxide.

Calcium Aluminate Hydrates

Hydration of C₃A/C₄AF, gypsum, ettringite and monosulphate.

C₃A Hydration

  • C₃A hydrates rapidly.
  • It contributes little to long-term strength.
  • Uncontrolled rapid reaction can cause rapid setting.
  • Gypsum is added during cement grinding to control the rapid reaction of C₃A.

Ettringite

  • Forms when C₃A reacts in the presence of sulphate.
  • It is calcium aluminate trisulphate hydrate.
  • It is commonly called ettringite.
  • It is normally one of the first products to hydrate and crystallise.

C₄AF

  • C₄AF is another aluminate/ferrite compound.
  • Its hydration products contribute relatively little to strength compared with calcium silicate hydrates.

C-S-H Morphology

  • C-S-H has a gel nature.
  • The reference describes porous gel, fibres and a poorly formed mass.
  • C-S-H is not a single sharply defined compound.

Structure, Transition Zone & Water

Structure of hydrated cement, transition zone, pores and water requirement.

Structure of Hydrated Cement

  • At macroscopic level, concrete can be considered as two phases: paste + aggregate.
  • At microscopic level, the transition zone forms a third phase.

Concrete — Simple Structure

AGGREGATE
TRANSITION
ZONE
CEMENT
PASTE

Transition Zone

  • Generally a region of weakness.
  • Its structure is influenced by bleeding.
  • Crystal size and concentration are important.
  • Drying shrinkage and temperature variation can cause microcracking.
  • Calcium hydroxide and ettringite crystals can be larger and more concentrated here than in bulk cement paste.
  • It is porous and can affect strength and durability.

Paste Structure and Water-Cement Ratio

  • Fresh paste is a plastic mass of cement and water.
  • During hydration, products form and the relationships between water, unhydrated cement, hydration products and capillary cavities change.
  • Water-cement ratio strongly affects capillary porosity, paste structure, strength and durability.

  • If water is less than about 38% by weight of cement, complete hydration cannot occur throughout the paste.

  • As hydration proceeds, products bridge spaces between cement particles.
  • Further hydration fills more space; remaining water may leave capillary cavities.
