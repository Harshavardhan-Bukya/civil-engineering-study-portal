TOPIC 01

Basics of Structural Engineering & Classification of Loads

Fundamental structural concepts and the complete classification of loads.

1. Structural Engineering

Structural Engineering │ ├── Analysis │ ├── EM – Displacement │ ├── SOM – Deformation │ └── SA – Structural Analysis │ └── Design ├── DRCS ├── SSD └── ASE

Structure

Definition: A structure is a combination of different members which transfers the applied loads safely to the supports.

Nature of Structure

  • Elastic nature: deformation disappears after the load is removed, provided the material remains within its elastic range.
  • Plastic nature: permanent deformation remains after removal of the load.

Structural Analysis

Structural analysis is used to determine the effects developed in a structure due to external loading.

  • Axial force
  • Shear force
  • Bending moment
  • Torsional moment / torque
  • Stress
  • Deformation / displacement

2. Classification of Loads

Loads are classified according to their nature, direction and manner of distribution.

A. With Respect to Time

Load │ ├── Static Load │ ├── Dead Load │ └── Gradually Applied Load │ └── Dynamic Load ├── Impact Load └── Fatigue / Repeated Load

Static Load

A load which is applied without significant acceleration or sudden impact is treated as a static load.

Dead Load

Dead load is the permanent load caused mainly by the self-weight of structural and permanently attached components.

Gradually Applied Load

A gradually applied load increases slowly from zero to its final value.

Dynamic Load

A dynamic load varies with time or is applied with acceleration, vibration or impact effects.

Impact Load

An impact load acts suddenly due to collision, falling weight or another impact action.

Fatigue / Repeated Load

Repeated or fluctuating loading may cause fatigue failure after a large number of stress cycles.

B. With Respect to Direction

Load │ ├── Normal Load │ ├── Tension │ └── Compression │ └── Shear Load ├── Direct / Transfer Shear └── Eccentric Shear

Normal Load

A normal load acts perpendicular to the resisting cross-sectional area.

Shear Load

A shear load acts parallel or tangential to the resisting area.

Eccentric Load / Eccentric Shear

When the line of action of the load does not pass through the relevant centroidal axis and has an eccentricity, additional moment effects are produced.

TOPIC 02

Stress

Stress, strength, classification of stress, stress at a point and three-dimensional stress components.

1. Stress

Definition: Stress is the internal resisting force developed per unit area due to the action of an external load.
AVERAGE NORMAL STRESS
σ = P / A

Where:

  • σ = normal stress
  • P = axial force
  • A = resisting cross-sectional area

Units of Stress

1 Pa = 1 N/m²
1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa
1 MPa = 1 N/mm²

2. Strength

Strength is the ability of a material to resist an applied load or stress without failure.

Strength is treated as a material property, whereas the actual stress developed in a member depends on loading and geometry.

Types of Strength

  • Tensile strength
  • Compressive strength
  • Shear strength

3. Classification of Stress

Stress │ ├── Direct Stress │ │ │ ├── Normal / Axial Stress │ │ ├── Tensile Stress │ │ └── Compressive Stress │ │ │ └── Direct Shear Stress │ └── Stress due to Moment ├── Bending Stress └── Torsional Shear Stress

Tensile Stress

Tensile stress is developed when the member is subjected to tensile axial force.

σt = +P/A 
← P  |==========================|  P →
              ΔL > 0

Compressive Stress

Compressive stress is developed when the member is subjected to compressive axial force.

σc = −P/A 
P →  |==========================|  ← P
              ΔL < 0
In the sign convention used here, tensile normal stress is positive and compressive normal stress is negative.

Shear Stress

Shear stress acts tangentially to the resisting area.
τ = V/A

Where V is the shear force and A is the resisting shear area for a uniform average shear-stress calculation.

4. Stress at a Point

Stress is fundamentally defined at a point. For an infinitesimal area element dA carrying an infinitesimal force dP:

σ = dP/dA
dP = σ dA

Over a finite area:

P = ∫A σ dA

If the normal stress is uniform over the entire area:

P = σA
∴ σ = P/A

5. Stress Components in Three Dimensions

At a point in a three-dimensional body, the state of stress is represented by normal and shear components.

Normal Stress Components

σx,   σy,   σz

Shear Stress Components

τxy, τxz, τyx, τyz, τzx, τzy

Thus, when written on the three mutually perpendicular faces, there are three normal-stress components and six shear-stress components.

TOPIC 03

Strain

Longitudinal, lateral, volumetric and shear strain with derivations and worked examples.

1. Strain

Definition: Strain is the ratio of change in a dimension to the corresponding original dimension.
Strain = Change in Dimension / Original Dimension
Strain is dimensionless because it is a ratio of quantities having the same dimensions.

Strain can be measured experimentally using a strain gauge.

Classification of Strain

Strain │ ├── Normal Strain │ ├── Longitudinal / Linear Strain │ ├── Lateral Strain │ └── Volumetric Strain │ └── Shear Strain

2. Longitudinal Strain

Longitudinal strain is the ratio of change in length to the original length in the longitudinal direction.
εL = ΔL/L

Tensile Strain

εt = +ΔL/L

Compressive Strain

εc = −ΔL/L

3. Lateral Strain

Lateral strain is the strain produced in a direction perpendicular to the longitudinal direction of loading.
εlat = Change in Lateral Dimension / Original Lateral Dimension

For a circular member:

εd = Δd/d

4. Volumetric Strain

Volumetric strain is the ratio of change in volume to original volume.
εv = ΔV/V
The differential relations derived below are first-order small-strain relations. Products of small changes are neglected.

A. Rectangular Body

For dimensions L, B and H:

V = LBH

Differentiating:

dV = BH dL + LH dB + LB dH

Dividing by V = LBH:

dV/V = dL/L + dB/B + dH/H
∴ εv = εL + εB + εH

B. Cylindrical Body

V = πd²L/4

Taking the first-order differential:

dV/V = dL/L + 2(dd/d)
∴ εv = εL + 2εd

C. Spherical Body

V = πd³/6

Differentiating:

dV = (πd²/2) dd

Therefore:

dV/V = 3(dd/d)
∴ εv = 3εd

5. Shear Strain

Shear strain is the angular distortion produced by shear deformation.
Shear Deformation
Before deformation        After deformation

B ┌──────── C             B'──────── C'
  │                         /
  │                        /
  │                       /
A └──────── D             A──────── D'

Top displacement = Δx
Height           = h
Angular change   = φ
tan φ = Δx/h

For a small angle measured in radians:

tan φ ≈ φ
∴ γ ≈ φ ≈ Δx/h

Unit: radian; however, shear strain is fundamentally dimensionless.

6. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Cylindrical Rod

Given:

Initial length, L = 1 m
Final length = 1.3 m
Initial diameter, d = 8 mm
Final diameter = 10 mm

Longitudinal strain:

εL = (1.3 − 1)/1 = 0.3

Diameter strain:

εd = (10 − 8)/8 = 0.25

Using the small-strain volumetric relation written in the notes:

εv = εL + 2εd
εv = 0.3 + 2(0.25) = 0.8
Answer according to the small-strain relation used in the notes: εv = 0.8
The dimensional changes in this numerical are large. Therefore εv = εL + 2εd is being retained specifically as the small-strain relation used in the notes; it should not be treated as an exact finite-change volume calculation.

Example 2 — Sphere

Initial diameter = 15 cm
Final diameter = 20 cm

εd = (20 − 15)/15 = 1/3

Using the small-strain relation:

εv = 3εd
εv = 3 × (1/3) = 1
Answer according to the small-strain relation used in the notes: εv = 1
As the diameter change is large, the differential relation εv = 3εd is not an exact finite-change volume calculation.

Example 3 — Shear Strain

Height = 1 m = 100 cm
Horizontal displacement = 10 cm

γ ≈ Δx/h
γ ≈ 10/100 = 0.1 rad
Shear strain ≈ 0.1 rad

TOPIC 04

Principles, Assumptions & Design Criteria

Material assumptions, superposition, Saint-Venant's principle, Hooke's law, axial deformation, stiffness and design requirements.

1. Basic Assumptions

  1. The material is treated as homogeneous.
  2. The material is treated as isotropic.
  3. The member is prismatic where the corresponding formula requires it.
  4. Loading is static / gradually applied for the elementary relations.
  5. Hooke's law is used within the linear elastic range.
  6. The material is treated as a continuum.
  7. Superposition is applicable for linear-elastic behaviour.
  8. Self-weight may be neglected when specified or insignificant.
  9. For pure axial loading, the load passes through the centroidal axis. Eccentric loading introduces additional moment/bending effects.
  10. Saint-Venant's principle is used sufficiently far away from localized load application and geometric discontinuities.

2. Homogeneous Material

A homogeneous material has the same material properties from one point to another throughout the body.

3. Isotropic Material

An isotropic material has the same material properties in every direction at a point.
Homogeneity concerns variation from point to point. Isotropy concerns variation with direction.

4. Principle of Superposition

In a linear-elastic system, the total response caused by several loads is equal to the algebraic sum of the responses produced by the loads acting separately.

δ = δ₁ + δ₂ + δ₃ + ...

5. Saint-Venant's Principle

The exact manner in which a load is distributed over a small region strongly affects stresses near the load application point.

At sections sufficiently far from that region, statically equivalent load distributions produce essentially the same stress distribution.

6. Design Criteria

Design Criteria │ ├── Strength ├── Stiffness ├── Strain Energy └── Stability

Strength Criterion

The stress developed in the member must remain within the permissible or allowable stress/strength specified for the design.

Stiffness Criterion

Deformation must remain within the permissible limit.

k = P/δ

Strain-Energy Criterion

Strain energy is the energy stored in a body due to deformation. Energy absorption/storage requirements may form part of the design criterion.

Stability Criterion

A structure must remain stable against undesirable instability, overturning or buckling-type behaviour as applicable.

7. Hooke's Law

Within the proportional limit, stress is directly proportional to strain.
σ ∝ ε
σ = Eε
E = σ/ε

E is Young's modulus or modulus of elasticity.

8. Axial Deformation of a Uniform Prismatic Bar

σ = P/A
ε = δ/L

From Hooke's law:

E = σ/ε

Substituting:

E = (P/A) / (δ/L)
E = PL/(Aδ)

Therefore:

δ = PL/(AE)

This relation applies to a uniform prismatic member subjected to constant axial load with linear-elastic behaviour.

9. Axial Stiffness

k = P/δ

Using:

δ = PL/(AE)

Therefore:

k = AE/L

Hence axial stiffness:

  • increases with cross-sectional area A,
  • increases with Young's modulus E,
  • decreases with member length L.

10. Worked Example — Axial Extension

Given:

P = 10 kN
L = 1 m
d = 10 mm
E = 2 × 10⁵ N/mm²

Convert units:

P = 10 × 1000 = 10,000 N
L = 1 × 1000 = 1000 mm

Area:

A = πd²/4
A = π(10)²/4 = 78.54 mm²

Axial extension:

δ = PL/(AE)
δ = (10,000 × 1000) / (78.54 × 2 × 10⁵)
δ ≈ 0.637 mm
Final Answer: δ ≈ 0.637 mm

📌 Main Formulas

σ = Eε
E = σ/ε
δ = PL/(AE)
k = P/δ
k = AE/L
δ = δ₁ + δ₂ + δ₃ + ...

TOPIC 05

Mechanical Properties of Materials

Strength, stiffness, elasticity, plasticity, ductility, malleability, brittleness, hardness, resilience, toughness, fatigue and creep.

1. Strength

Strength is the ability of a material to resist applied stress without failure.

2. Stiffness

Stiffness is the ability of a member to resist deformation.
k = P/δ

For a uniform axially loaded prismatic bar:

k = AE/L

3. Elasticity

Elasticity is the property by which a material regains its original shape and dimensions after removal of load, provided it has not been loaded beyond its elastic range.

4. Plasticity

Plasticity is the ability of a material to undergo permanent deformation without rupture and retain the deformed shape after unloading.

5. Malleability

Malleability is the ability of a material to undergo plastic deformation mainly under compressive loading and to be formed into thin sheets.

Examples include gold, silver and aluminium.

6. Ductility

Ductility is the ability of a material to undergo considerable plastic deformation under tensile loading before fracture.

A ductile material can be drawn into wires.

Mild steel is a common example of a ductile engineering material.

7. Brittleness

Brittleness is the tendency of a material to fracture with little plastic deformation.

Examples include glass, ceramics and concrete.

8. Hardness

Hardness is the resistance of a material to localized plastic deformation, indentation, penetration or scratching.

Hardness Tests

  1. Brinell hardness test
  2. Rockwell hardness test
  3. Vickers hardness test

9. Resilience

Resilience is the capacity of a material to absorb and store strain energy within the elastic range.

On a stress–strain diagram, the elastic strain energy per unit volume corresponds to the area under the elastic portion of the curve.

10. Toughness

Toughness is the capacity of a material to absorb energy up to fracture.

It is represented by the total area under the stress–strain curve up to fracture.

11. Fatigue

Fatigue is progressive damage and possible failure caused by repeated, fluctuating or reversing stresses.

Fatigue failure may occur after a large number of loading cycles.

Endurance Strength / Endurance Limit

Endurance behaviour describes the resistance of a material to repeated cyclic stresses.

12. Creep

Creep is progressive time-dependent deformation under sustained stress or load, particularly important at elevated temperature.

13. Ductile and Brittle Behaviour

Ductile Material Brittle Material
Large plastic deformation before fracture Little plastic deformation before fracture
Noticeable deformation gives warning before failure Failure may occur relatively suddenly
Example: Mild steel Examples: Concrete, glass, ceramics

14. RCC Concept

Concrete has high compressive strength but comparatively low tensile strength.

In reinforced cement concrete, steel reinforcement is provided mainly to resist tensile stresses, while concrete is particularly effective in compression.

TOPIC 06

Mild Steel Stress–Strain Curve

Tensile testing, UTM, proportional and elastic limits, yielding, strain hardening, ultimate tensile stress, necking and fracture.

1. Tensile Test of Mild Steel

A tensile test is performed to study the mechanical behaviour of a material under gradually increasing tensile load.

Universal Testing Machine — UTM

A Universal Testing Machine is used to apply and measure the tensile load on the specimen.

Extensometer

An extensometer is used to measure extension over a specified gauge length.

Engineering Stress = P/A₀
Engineering Strain = ΔL/L₀

3. O–A: Linear Proportional Region

In this region, stress is directly proportional to strain.

σ ∝ ε
σ = Eε
E = σ/ε

The slope of the straight portion of the stress–strain curve is Young's modulus.

4. Proportional Limit — Point A

Point A represents the limit up to which stress remains proportional to strain.

5. Elastic Limit — Point B

Up to the elastic limit, the specimen can regain its original dimensions after unloading.

6. Upper and Lower Yield Points

Upper Yield Point — C′

Mild steel first reaches the upper yield point, after which the engineering stress drops.

Lower Yield Point — C

After the upper yield point, the stress falls to the lower yield level. Significant plastic strain can occur with relatively little change in engineering stress.

7. Strain Hardening

After the yielding stage, further plastic deformation requires an increase in stress. This behaviour is called strain hardening.

During strain hardening, the engineering stress rises toward the ultimate tensile stress.

8. Ultimate Tensile Stress — Point E

Ultimate tensile stress is the maximum engineering stress reached by the specimen during the tensile test.
UTS = Pmax/A₀

After the ultimate point, localized reduction in cross-sectional area begins.

9. Necking

Necking is the localized reduction in cross-sectional area that occurs after the ultimate tensile stress is reached in a ductile specimen.

10. Fracture / Breaking Point — F

Continued necking ultimately causes the specimen to fracture.

Point F represents the fracture or breaking point on the engineering stress–strain curve.

11. Complete Sequence

Linear Proportional Behaviour ↓ Proportional Limit ↓ Elastic Limit ↓ Upper Yield Point ↓ Lower Yield Point ↓ Yielding / Plastic Flow ↓ Strain Hardening ↓ Ultimate Tensile Stress ↓ Necking ↓ Fracture
