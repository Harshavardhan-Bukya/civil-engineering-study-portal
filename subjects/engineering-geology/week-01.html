TOPIC 01

Branches of Geology & Importance in Civil Engineering

The branches of geology introduced in the Week 01 class notes and their engineering significance.

1. Physical Geology

Definition: Physical Geology is described in the class notes as Dynamic Geology or Geomorphology and deals with the different physical features of the Earth.

It deals with physical features such as:

  • Mountains
  • Rivers
  • Lakes
  • Glaciers
  • Volcanoes

It also deals with changes occurring on the Earth's surface, including the formation or disappearance of rivers, springs and lakes.

Natural phenomena considered include:

  • Slides and landslides
  • Earthquakes
  • Weathering

The geological work of wind, glaciers, oceans and groundwater continuously modifies the Earth's surface.

⭐ Exam Point: Physical Geology deals with physical features of the Earth and processes that modify its surface.

2. Mineralogy

Mineralogy deals with the study of minerals.

It includes the study of:

  • Formation
  • Composition
  • Occurrence
  • Types
  • Associated properties
  • Uses of minerals

3. Petrology

Petro = Rock
Logy = Study

Therefore, Petrology is the study of rocks.

The Earth's crust is called the lithosphere and is made up of different types of rocks.

Petrology deals with:

  • Formation
  • Structure
  • Features
  • Composition
  • Occurrence
  • Types of rocks

4. Structural Geology

Structural Geology deals with rocks forming the Earth's crust that undergo deformation, distortion and disturbances under the influence of tectonic forces.

These result in geological structures such as:

  • Folds
  • Faults
  • Joints
  • Unconformities
Supporting detail added for clarity: Tectonic forces are forces associated with movement and deformation of the Earth's crust.
⭐ Faults, folds and joints are particularly important in engineering because they can affect the stability of rock masses.

5. Historical Geology

The Earth's surface provides suitable conditions for deposition of sediments at different places. Sedimentary rocks can therefore preserve evidence representing different periods of Earth's history.

Investigation of these rocks can reveal the chronological sequence of:

  • Formation of rocks
  • Evolution of life
  • Migration of plants and animals
  • Life during different periods of Earth's history
The study of Earth's history through geological records, particularly sedimentary rocks, is called Historical Geology.

6. Palaeontology

Under favourable conditions, animals and plants may become embedded in sediments and may be preserved partly or completely.

Naturally preserved remnants or evidence of ancient life occurring in rocks are known as fossils.

Palaeontology deals with:

  • Mode of formation of fossils
  • Types of fossils
  • Occurrence of fossils
Supporting detail added for clarity: A fossil is preserved evidence or remains of organisms that lived in the geological past.

7. Economic Geology

The prosperity of a nation depends to a considerable extent on economically useful mineral deposits.

Economic Geology deals with geological materials and mineral deposits having economic importance.

Example from the notes: Countries rich in oil deposits.

8. Engineering Geology

⭐ Very Important
Engineering Geology deals with the application of geological knowledge in the field of Civil Engineering for execution of safe, stable and economical constructions such as dams, bridges and tunnels.
  • Dams
  • Bridges
  • Tunnels

9. Mining Geology

Mining Geology deals with the application of geological knowledge in the field of mining.

A mining engineer should know:

  • Mode of occurrence of ores
  • Associations of ores
  • Properties of ores

Important physical parameters mentioned in the notes are:

  • Depth
  • Direction — Strike
  • Inclination — Dip
  • Thickness
Supporting detail added for clarity: Strike describes the direction of a geological plane, while dip describes its inclination from the horizontal.

10. Geophysics

Geophysics deals with the Earth's crust and physical properties of the Earth such as density and magnetism.

Classification

Pure Geophysics

Deals with general aspects of the Earth as a whole.

Exploration Geophysics

Deals with investigation of the upper layers of the Earth's crust.

11. Geo-hydrology / Hydrogeology

Geo-hydrology is also called Hydrogeology.

It deals with groundwater, including its:

  • Occurrence
  • Movement
  • Nature
  • Quality
  • Quantity

Groundwater behaviour is related to properties such as:

  • Porosity
  • Permeability
  • Structure
  • Texture
  • Composition of geological material

12. Geochemistry

Geochemistry deals with the chemical composition of minerals and rocks.

It also deals with:

  • Occurrence
  • Distribution
  • Abundance
  • Mobility

⭐ Importance of Geology in Civil Engineering

  1. Before construction, proper site selection is important from the point of view of foundation stability.
  2. Geology provides systematic knowledge of construction materials and their properties.
  3. Knowledge about the nature of rocks is useful in tunnelling and construction of roads.
  4. Foundation problems of dams, bridges and buildings are directly related to the geology of the area.
  5. Knowledge of groundwater is necessary for excavation works, water supply, irrigation and other purposes.
  6. Knowledge of erosion, transportation and deposition by surface water helps in soil conservation and river control.
  7. Geological maps and sections considerably help in planning engineering projects.
  8. Geological features such as faults, folds and joints must be suitably treated to maintain stability of rock structures.
  9. A pre-geological survey of the concerned area reduces the cost of planning work.

TOPIC 02

Geological Agents

Geological agents and the distinction between exogenous and endogenous processes.

Geological Agents

Geological agents are natural forces or processes responsible for visible/geological changes on or within the Earth.

Classification

1. Exogenous Geological Agents

Agents/processes acting mainly at or near the Earth's surface.

2. Endogenous Geological Agents

Agents/processes originating below the Earth's surface.

1. Exogenous Geological Agents

According to the notes, agents originating at the Earth's surface work slowly but steadily and erase topographical irregularities or relief.

Their geological work occurs systematically through:

Erosion → Transportation → Deposition

Erosion

Erosion involves removal or wearing away of earth/rock material.

Transportation

Transportation is the movement of eroded material from one place to another.

Deposition

Deposition is the settling or accumulation of transported material.

The notes refer to rivers and their large-scale transportation of sediments as an example of geological work.

⭐ Remember the sequence: Erosion → Transportation → Deposition

2. Endogenous Geological Agents

The nature, origin and function of endogenous geological agents are opposite to surface-originating exogenous agents.

They originate below the Earth's surface and can act suddenly, creating topographical irregularities.

Examples mentioned around this section of the notes include:

  • Volcanoes
  • Earthquakes
  • Tectonic forces
  • Tsunamis
Clarification: Groundwater is also written in the example line in the notes. In standard geological classification, groundwater is normally treated as an external geological agent. It is therefore preserved as a note reference here but is not classified as an endogenous process in this portal.
2 MARKS

Define geological agents and give their classification.

Answer: Geological agents are natural forces/processes responsible for geological changes on or within the Earth. They are classified into (1) Exogenous geological agents and (2) Endogenous geological agents.
3 MARKS

Answer:
  1. Erosion: Removal or wearing away of earth/rock material.
  2. Transportation: Movement of eroded material.
  3. Deposition: Settling or accumulation of transported material.

Sequence: Erosion → Transportation → Deposition.

Answer:

Tend to reduce or erase topographical irregularities. Can create topographical irregularities.

TOPIC 03

Weathering of Rocks

Mechanical, Chemical and Biological Weathering from the Week 01 handwritten notes.

Weathering of Rocks

Weathering is the process by which rocks are broken down and decomposed by the action of natural agencies such as wind, rivers, rain and temperature changes.

Classification of Weathering

  1. Mechanical Weathering
  2. Chemical Weathering
  3. Biological Weathering
Weathering → Mechanical • Chemical • Biological

A. Mechanical Weathering

Mechanical weathering involves the physical breakdown of rocks into smaller pieces due to natural forces.

The types listed in the notes are:

  1. Frost Wedging
  2. Expansion and Contraction
  3. Effect of Vegetation

1. Frost Wedging

Water present in cracks of rocks freezes during cold conditions and melts when temperature rises.

Freezing of water causes an increase in volume. This produces pressure against the walls of the rock cracks.

Repeated freezing and thawing gradually widens the cracks and ultimately causes the rock to break down.

Water enters cracks → Freezing → Expansion → Pressure → Crack widening → Rock breakdown

2. Expansion and Contraction

Solar radiation causes heating of rocks during daytime and produces thermal expansion.

During night, temperature falls and the rocks contract.

Expansion and contraction are mainly confined to the surface layers of rocks. Repeated heating and cooling develops stresses in these layers and may result in fracture and mechanical disintegration.

Day heating → Expansion → Night cooling → Contraction → Repetition → Stress → Fracture

3. Effect of Vegetation

During the growth of vegetation in rocky terrain, roots penetrate existing weak planes, joints and cracks.

As roots continue to grow and become thicker, they exert pressure on the crack walls and gradually widen the cracks, resulting in physical breakdown of the rock mass.

Roots enter cracks → Root growth → Pressure → Crack widening → Rock breakdown

B. Chemical Weathering

Chemical weathering involves chemical reactions that alter rocks and produce new alteration products. Water is the principal fluid directly affecting rocks in these processes.

The processes written in the notes are:

  1. Resolution
  2. Leaching
  3. Hydration

1. Resolution

In carbonate rocks such as limestone, dolomite and marble, river water travelling through or over the rocks dissolves carbonate material.

The dissolved material is removed, resulting in reduction in the size or mass of the rock.

Water + Carbonate rock → Carbonate dissolves → Material removed → Rock size/mass reduces

2. Leaching

Leaching is the removal of soluble content from rocks by water.

Water is a powerful leaching agent and affects soluble materials that come in contact with it.

Water contacts rock → Soluble content dissolves → Dissolved material is removed

3. Hydration

Hydration is the process in which water molecules are incorporated into the molecular structure of minerals, bringing about decomposition or alteration of minerals.
Water molecules enter mineral structure → Structural/property change → Alteration or decomposition

C. Biological Weathering

Biological weathering is the breakdown or decomposition of rocks due to the activities of living organisms.

The notes mention organisms such as bacteria and fungi.

Acids produced by organisms can cause decomposition of rocks. Microorganisms may also penetrate mineral crystals and remove specific ions from internal layers.

Living organisms → Acids / biological action → Mineral attack → Ion removal / decomposition → Rock breakdown

TOPIC 04

Structure of the Earth

Atmosphere, Lithosphere, Crust, Mantle and Core from the Week 01 handwritten notes.

Earth's Structure

The notes describe the atmosphere and the internal structure of the Earth, including the lithosphere, crust, mantle and core.

Earth → Atmosphere + Lithosphere / Interior → Crust → Mantle → Core

A. Atmosphere

The atmosphere is the outer gaseous part or gaseous envelope surrounding the Earth.

The notes describe it as extending to about 700 km and even beyond.

  1. Troposphere
  2. Stratosphere
  3. Mesosphere
  4. Thermosphere

1. Troposphere

The troposphere is the lowermost atmospheric zone and begins at the Earth's surface.

The notes give an average height of about 20 km, with the upper boundary about 9 km above the poles and about 18 km above the equator.

2. Stratosphere

The stratosphere is the second atmospheric layer. It begins above the troposphere and extends to about 50 km.

The notes place the ozone layer approximately from 9 km to 35 km above the Earth's surface.

3. Mesosphere

The mesosphere is the third atmospheric zone. It starts at about 50 km and continues to about 85 km.

It is characterised by a steep fall in temperature, reaching about −100°C in the notes.

4. Thermosphere

The thermosphere is the last atmospheric zone listed in the notes. It starts at about 85 km and extends to about 690 km.

Temperature rises again and may reach 1000°C and above.

B. Lithosphere

The notes identify the lithosphere as the upper solid region/shell of the Earth associated with the crust and uppermost rigid part.

1. Crust

The crust is the uppermost solid shell of the Earth and has different thicknesses in different areas.
LocationThickness in notes
Under oceans5–6 km
Under continents30–35 km
Under mountains60–70 km

Oceanic Crust

Oceanic crust consists mainly of dense, dark-coloured rocks such as basalt.

Continental Crust

Continental crust forms the continents and consists mainly of less-dense, lighter-coloured rocks such as granite.

C. Mantle

The mantle is the layer below the crust and above the core.

The notes place it approximately from 60 km to 2900 km depth and describe it as about 2900 km thick.

The notes record the mantle as making up about 67% of the Earth's mass.

Pressure and temperature increase with depth. The temperature range written in the notes is approximately 870°C to 2900°C.

D. Core

The core is the innermost layer of the Earth.
  • It occupies about one-third of the Earth's mass.
  • The notes record about 15% of the Earth's volume.
  • The notes give a core diameter of about 6800 km.
  • Temperature is written as approximately 2000°C to 7000°C.
  • It is divided into the Outer Core and Inner Core.

1. Outer Core

The notes place the outer core approximately between 2900 km and 5000 km depth.

It is described as a molten liquid metallic layer, mainly iron and nickel.

Its thickness is written as about 2200 km.

2. Inner Core

The notes place the inner core approximately from 5000 km to 6370 km.

It is described as a dense solid body composed mainly of iron and nickel.

The notes mention the extreme pressure in this region.

TOPIC 05

Plate Tectonics

Plate Tectonics Theory, plate boundaries and Subduction Zone from the Week 01 handwritten notes.

Plate Tectonics Theory

The Earth's crust and upper mantle are broken into sections called plates. These plates move relative to one another over/around the mantle.

Plate movements in the notes

  • Plates collide → Convergent Boundary
  • Plates separate → Divergent Boundary
  • Plates slide horizontally past one another → Transform Boundary
Subduction Zone: a region where one tectonic plate descends beneath another plate.

1. Convergent Boundary

At a convergent boundary, two tectonic plates move towards one another.

Compression occurs. Depending on the plates involved, one plate may descend beneath another, or collision may produce deformation and mountain building.

The notes connect continental collision with formation of mountain ranges.

Examples written in the notes

  • Andes Mountains, South America: associated with convergence of the Nazca Plate and South American Plate.
  • Himalayas: associated with collision of the Indian/Indo-Australian plate region with the Eurasian Plate.

Subduction Zone

A subduction zone is a region at a convergent boundary where one plate sinks/descends beneath another.
Plates converge → One plate bends downward → Plate descends beneath another → Subduction Zone

2. Divergent Boundary

At a divergent boundary, tectonic plates move away from each other.

As plates separate, magma rises or is extruded from the mantle to fill the void between them.

The magma cools and solidifies to form new crust.

Examples written in the notes

  • Mid-Atlantic Ridge
  • Great Rift Valley in Eastern Africa
Plates pull apart → Gap develops → Magma rises → Magma cools → New crust forms

3. Transform Boundary

At a transform boundary, tectonic plates slide horizontally past one another.

The notes describe these boundaries as conservative because material along the margins is neither created nor destroyed.

Transform movement is associated with faulting.

Example in the notes: San Andreas Fault.
