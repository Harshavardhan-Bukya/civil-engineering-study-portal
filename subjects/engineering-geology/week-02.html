Mineralogy Basics & Mineral Identification

Definition of mineralogy, definition of minerals and physical-property identification.

Define Mineralogy

Mineralogy deals with the study of minerals. It deals with the detailed mode of formation, composition, occurrence, types, association, properties, uses etc.

Definition of Mineral

A mineral is a naturally-occurring, homogeneous, solid with a definite, but generally not fixed, chemical composition and an ordered atomic arrangement. It is usually formed by inorganic processes.

1. Natural

Occurs naturally and is not man-made.

2. Homogeneous

It is the same throughout and cannot be broken into simpler components.

3. Solid

Minerals maintain a set shape nearly indefinitely. Liquids are not minerals.

4. Chemical Composition

A mineral can be described by a chemical formula.

5. Ordered Arrangement of Atoms

Minerals have a fixed atomic pattern that repeats over a large region.

6. Generally Inorganic

Most minerals are inorganic. Only a few organic substances are considered minerals.

Common modes of occurrence and forms of minerals.

The form represents the common mode of occurrence of a mineral in nature. It is also called the habit or structure of minerals. To some extent, it is a function of the atomic structure of minerals.

Colour

Colour as a physical property used in mineral identification.

Colour of Minerals

Colour is due to the composition of the mineral. In some minerals, colour is imparted by trace elements, inclusions and atomic structure.

Great consistency in ore-forming minerals is referred to as Idiochromatic. Less consistency in rock-forming minerals is referred to as Allochromatic.

Mineral Colour stated in source
GraphiteShining black
HematiteDark steel grey
AmethystViolet
CalciteWhite
JasperRed
OlivineOlive green
QuartzColourless or white
AsbestosWhite, less commonly green, yellow, grey
PyrolusiteDark grey, nearly black
GalenaDark lead grey
BarytesWhite or pale grey

Colour and Chemical Composition

  • Minerals rich in Al, Ca, Na and Mg are often light coloured.
  • Minerals rich in Fe, Ti, Ni and Cr are often dark in colour.

Colour and Atomic Structure

Atomic structure controls which components of white light are absorbed or reflected.

  • White minerals reflect all components of white light.
  • Black minerals absorb all components of white light.
  • Green minerals reflect green light and absorb the others.
Limitation: Colour is not particularly useful as a diagnostic property because some minerals show a wide variety of colours and many minerals have similar colours.

Streak

Colour of mineral powder obtained by rubbing the mineral on a streak plate.

Definition

Streak is the colour of a mineral's powder obtained by rubbing a mineral specimen on an unglazed white porcelain tile.

  • Useful for identifying metallic ore minerals.
  • Silicates generally do not mark the tile and have no streak.
  • White minerals streaked on a white tile will have a white streak.
  • Minerals harder than the tile (6) have no streak.

Lustre

The way in which a mineral reflects light.

Definition

Lustre is the way in which a mineral reflects light. It is controlled by the atomic structure of the mineral. It represents the nature of shining on the surface of minerals.

Based on the quality or type of shining, lustres are grouped as metallic and non-metallic.

Fracture

Breaking of minerals along irregular or curved surfaces.

Definition

Fracture is the tendency of minerals to break along a flat surface or to break unevenly along a curved surface or irregular surface.

Fracture occurs where bond strength is generally the same in all directions. Minerals that have fracture do not exhibit cleavage.

Types of Fracture

Type Description Example stated in source
Even Fracture Broken surface is plain and smooth. Magnesite, Chalk
Uneven Fracture Broken surface is rough or irregular. Sodalite
Hackly Fracture Broken surface is very irregular like a broken stick. Kyanite, Asbestos, Tremolite
Conchoidal Fracture Broken surface is smooth and curved. Volcanic glass, Opal
Sub-conchoidal Fracture Surface is smooth and curved, but curved nature is less predominant. Flint, Agate, Jasper

Cleavage

Definite direction or plane along which minerals break easily.

Definition

Cleavage is the definite direction or plane along which a mineral tends to break easily.

It is related to crystallinity. Only crystalline minerals have cleavage. Cleavage represents the plane of weakness in the atomic structure of minerals.

Hardness

Resistance offered by minerals to abrasion or scratching.

Definition

Hardness is the resistance offered by minerals to abrasion or scratching. It is related to the atomic structure of minerals.

The source notes that chemical composition appears to have less influence over hardness.

Absolute and Relative Hardness

  • Absolute hardness: Total hardness.
  • Relative hardness: Comparative hardness.

Specific Gravity / Density

Ratio of mass of a substance to the mass of a reference substance for the same volume.

Definition

Specific gravity is the ratio of the mass of a substance to the mass of a reference substance for the same given volume.

Specific gravity of minerals depends on their chemical composition and atomic structure.

The source states that specific gravity of minerals can be determined using either Walker's steel yard or Jolly's spring.

Degree of Transparency

Resistance offered by materials to the passage of light.

Definition

Degree of transparency is the resistance offered by materials to the passage of light through them.

  • Transparency depends on chemical composition.
  • Ore minerals exhibit opaque behaviour.
  • Degree of transparency mainly depends on thickness.
Type Meaning / Example
Transparent Light can pass through clearly. Examples: Muscovite mica, Quartz.
Translucent Light passes through but not clearly. Examples: Calcite, Agate.
Opaque Light does not pass through. Examples shown include Galena and opaque mineral specimens.
Transparent → Translucent → Opaque

Special Properties & Importance of Minerals

Special identifying properties and importance in Civil Engineering.

Special Properties

Some minerals exhibit peculiar characters which enable them to be identified easily.

Mineral Special property stated in source
Talc Very soft (h=1); exhibits smooth touch or soapy feel.
Graphite Low hardness; black colour; marks easily on paper.
Realgar Gives garlic smell when struck or heated and on a freshly broken surface.
Orpiment Associated with the garlic-smell observation in the source.
Kaolin Gives a clayey smell and adheres strongly to the tongue.
Halite Has a saline taste.
Magnetic mineral specimen shown in source Strongly attracted by an ordinary magnet.

Importance of Minerals

The solid Earth consists of rocks and rocks are made of minerals. Understanding minerals helps in understanding rocks.

Civil engineers need to know the properties of rocks precisely so that different rocks can be considered for purposes such as:

  • Foundation rocks
  • Road metals
  • Building stone

Minerals are also heavily used in manufacturing plastics, clothes, pencil lead, glass, chalk board, chalk and salts.

Minerals serve as raw materials for manufacturing chemicals, dimension stone, aggregates for roads and concrete.

Civil Engineering Importance: Knowledge of minerals helps in understanding the properties and uses of rocks for foundations, roads and building materials.

Rock Forming Minerals & Silicate Structures

Rock-forming minerals, chemical composition of Earth's crust, silicate structures and important mineral groups.

Rock Forming Minerals

Based on nature and economic importance, minerals are grouped into rock-forming and economic-forming minerals. Some economic minerals serve as sources for extraction of valuable metals, while others become useful because of their physical properties.

The source states that about 99% of the Earth's crust is made up of about 20–25 rock-forming minerals.

The source states that 99.2% of the Earth's crust is made of these nine elements and oxygen and silicate together constitute 74.4%.

Rock-forming minerals are mainly silicates, although oxides and carbonates are also included.

The silicate tetrahedron is the fundamental unit of all silicate minerals. It may occur alone or in different combinations within mineral structures.

Rock Forming Mineral Groups

Group Important points from source
Feldspar Group Feldspar is not a single mineral. Aluminium silicates of sodium, potassium and calcium, rarely barium. Alkali and lime feldspar. All feldspars are tectosilicates. Occurs more commonly in metamorphic rocks.
Quartz Group Quartz is the most common rock-forming mineral. Silica composition. Silica occurs in crystalline nature. All quartz are tectosilicates. Occurs more commonly in igneous rocks.
Pyroxene Group Related to feldspar and mafic minerals. Silicates of calcium, magnesium and ferrous iron. Single-chain silicate. Occurs more commonly in igneous rocks.
Olivine Group Related to mafic minerals. Silicates of magnesium and ferrous iron. Olivine are nesosilicates. Occurs more commonly in igneous rocks.
Amphibole Group Related to ferro-magnesium minerals. Silicates of calcium, magnesium, sodium and ferrous iron. Inosilicates with double-chain silicates. Occurs more commonly in igneous rocks.
Mica Group Related to silicate minerals. Silicates of aluminium, magnesium and potassium. Mica are phyllosilicates. Occurs more commonly in igneous rocks.
Kyanite Group Related to metamorphic minerals. Silicates of aluminium, magnesium and potassium. Kyanite are nesosilicates. Occurs more commonly in metamorphic rocks.
Talc Group Extremely soft, non-metallic economic mineral. Hydrous magnesium silicates. Talc are phyllosilicates. Occurs more commonly in metamorphic rocks.
